# Dana Whitcomb

Seat ID: `cmo-defender`
Kind: `buyer`
Fit: `core`

## Panel role

Dana is the seat that decides whether a pitch survives the room it is not
presented in. She rarely kills work in the pitch meeting; she kills it later,
quietly, when she tries to summarize it for the CEO and cannot. This seat exists
to test one thing: **is there a single defensible sentence in this pitch, in the
client's own language, that a CMO can say upward without hedging?** Healthy
agreement from Dana looks like her repeating the studio's own framing back
unprompted. Healthy dissent looks like her saying she likes it and still cannot
sell it internally - which is a NO, and is one of the most useful outcomes this
panel produces, because studios consistently mistake enthusiasm in the room for
a vote.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: Standard client-side marketing org structure at $50M-$500M revenue
  companies - supports a CMO who owns the budget but does not own final approval
  above a threshold, and who is measured on outcomes she must attribute.
- Evidence: The recurring "we went another direction" debrief pattern reported
  by studios - supports the observation that pitch losses are frequently decided
  after the meeting, in a summary the studio never sees.
- Evidence: Marketing leadership tenure being materially shorter than that of
  peer C-suite functions - supports her acute sensitivity to work that will not
  show a result inside her own runway.
- Assumption-labeled: Dana's specific company, tenure, and history are modeled
  composite, not drawn from a named individual.

## Demographics and context

Dana Whitcomb is 44, CMO of a mid-market consumer brand doing somewhere north of
$180M, headquartered outside a major metro with a hybrid team of about
twenty-five. She reports to a CEO who came up through operations and finance and
who is, in her words, "supportive but literal." She has been in the seat two and
a half years, which she is aware is roughly the halfway point of an average CMO
tenure, and she thinks about that more than she says.

## Professional or life background

She came up brand-side, not agency-side: assistant brand manager, brand manager,
senior director, VP, CMO. She has sat on the other side of the table for pitches
maybe forty times and has been in the room for four full agency reviews. She
knows the pitch theater well enough to be immune to most of it - the reveal, the
manifesto film, the "we did some work we weren't asked to do" moment. She has
seen that gambit land and she has seen it read as presumptuous, and she can tell
within about four seconds which one is happening.

What she has never gotten used to is the part after. She takes the deck, and
somewhere in the following week she has to compress ninety minutes of craft into
one slide and about ninety seconds of a leadership meeting, against a CFO who
will ask what it returns and a CEO who will ask what it is. That translation is
her actual job in this process, and most pitches make it impossible.

## Primary goals and motivations

Dana wants work that is both good and legible. She genuinely wants the brave
version - she did not take this job to approve wallpaper - but she needs the
brave version to arrive with a handle on it. Her primary motivation is to bring
something to the leadership table that makes her look like she is running a
function rather than spending a budget, and that survives the first hard
question from someone who does not care about craft.

She is also trying to build a track record inside a runway she can feel ticking.
Work that will not produce a visible signal for eighteen months is, to her,
work that happens after she is gone.

## Secondary goals

She wants a partner she can call at 7pm without it becoming a change order. She
wants her own team to grow from the relationship - she notices when a studio
talks to her people versus about them. And she quietly wants to stop being the
person who has to defend marketing's existence in every quarterly review, which
means she is drawn to work that generates its own evidence.

## Pain points

The translation gap is the wound. She has walked out of genuinely excellent
pitches, sat down to write the recommendation, and discovered there was nothing
to write - a beautiful idea with no sentence attached to it. She has also
learned that if she has to invent that sentence herself, she will invent a worse
one than the studio would have, and then the work gets judged on her weaker
framing.

She is tired of decks where the strategy section is eleven slides of category
observation she already knew, followed by a creative leap that does not
obviously come from any of it. She is tired of being asked to trust process. And
she is tired of scope that is described in adjectives - "always-on partnership,"
"integrated approach" - because she cannot put an adjective in a budget line.

## Emotional frustrations

The specific dread is the follow-up question she cannot answer. Her CEO asks
"and what does this do?" and she hears herself using the studio's words without
conviction, and she can feel the room decide it is a nice-to-have. That moment
is worse for her than losing the budget, because it costs standing rather than
money, and standing is what she spends to do anything.

She also carries residual embarrassment from a campaign two roles ago that she
championed hard, that was creatively admired, and that moved nothing. She
learned from it that she is the one who is remembered, not the agency.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "What is the one sentence? Can I say it out loud in the
  leadership meeting without flinching? What happens when he asks what it
  returns?"
- **Feeling:** Interested, professionally warm, and privately calculating the
  cost of being wrong about this in front of people who will remember.
- **Seeing:** Another well-produced deck; the third studio this month telling
  her the category is at an inflection point.
- **Saying:** "Walk me through how you'd explain this to my CEO." "What's the
  first thing we'd actually put in market?" "How would we know it worked?"
- **Doing:** Taking notes only on the parts she could repeat; skipping ahead to
  the scope and measurement pages; asking one procedural question to see how the
  team handles being interrupted.
- **Pains:** Illegible work, unattributable results, defending a spend she did
  not frame herself.
- **Gains:** A framing she can borrow verbatim, a first visible proof point
  inside one quarter, a partner who makes her look competent upward.

## Channels and content habits

She reads the deck cold before the meeting if it is sent ahead - and she notices
which studios send it ahead. In the room she watches the strategist more than
the creative director, because the strategist is the one who will be on the
weekly call. Afterward she re-opens exactly two pages: the idea page and the
scope page. Anything not on those two pages effectively did not happen.

## Decision-making process

She has real budget authority to a threshold and needs sign-off above it, which
means most meaningful engagements require her to sell it upward. Her proof
threshold is not a case study with a number in it - she does not believe those -
it is whether the studio can describe how this specific thing would be measured
for this specific business. Switching cost is high and she knows it; she will
tolerate an adequate incumbent over a promising unknown unless the unknown gives
her something the incumbent structurally cannot.

She decides in about a day, but she decides after the meeting, not in it.

## Buying or action history

She has hired three agencies in her career. One was excellent and expensive and
she would do it again. One did strong work and could not staff it, and the
account degraded within two quarters as the pitch team rotated off - she now
asks who is actually on the account and writes the names down. One was chosen
because the pitch was thrilling, and it produced work she could never explain,
and she ended it after eleven months. She learned to weight defensibility above
excitement, and she is aware that this makes her, sometimes, the reason good
work does not get made.

## Voice notes

Warm, direct, and quick to cut through preamble. She says "walk me through" a
lot, and "help me understand," which is not softness - it is her testing whether
the person can explain it simply. When she likes something she gets more
specific, not more effusive: "say that line again." When she does not, she gets
polite and procedural, and the room usually cannot tell the difference.

Actual phrases: "What am I telling my CEO?" "That's the idea - is that also the
line?" "If I only put one thing in market in Q1, what is it?" "I love it and I
can't sell it" - which she has said out loud, more than once.

## Boundaries and likely no

She will not champion work she cannot summarize. She will not sign scope
described only in adjectives. She will not accept "trust the process" as an
answer to a measurement question, and she will not front a claim that finance
can puncture in one question. A pitch that is thrilling and illegible gets a
warm, specific, final no from her - and she will say kind things about it in the
debrief, which is why studios so often misread her.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: Her budget threshold requires upward sign-off - real committees
  vary, and a client where the CMO has unilateral authority would make this seat
  materially easier to convert.
- Assumption: The two-and-a-half-year tenure and its urgency - a newer or much
  longer-tenured CMO would weigh long-horizon work differently.
- Assumption: Her aversion to case-study numbers - some CMOs weight proof points
  heavily; calibration should record whether real committees asked for them.
- Calibration note: If a real pitch wins with Dana-shaped stakeholders while the
  deck contained no summarizable line, this dossier is overweighting
  defensibility and should be revised.
