# Priya Raghunathan

Seat ID: `brand-manager`
Kind: `user`
Fit: `core`

## Panel role

Priya is the seat that has to live inside the work after everyone else has moved
on. She does not control the award, but she can poison it - a brand manager who
feels her system was disrespected raises objections in the debrief that land
harder than any procurement note, because she is the only person in the room who
will still be working on this in eighteen months. This seat tests whether the
pitch treats the client's existing brand as an asset to build on or as an
obstacle the studio politely intends to route around. Healthy agreement is her
saying the work extends what she already has. Healthy dissent is her flagging,
specifically, the slide where the studio abandoned the system without saying so.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: Standard client-side brand governance structure - supports a brand
  manager who owns guidelines, approves asset usage, and fields every internal
  request for exceptions, without owning agency selection.
- Evidence: The common pitch pattern of presenting redesigned or reinterpreted
  brand assets as a demonstration of ambition - supports her documented
  ambivalence, since the same gesture reads as either vision or as a studio that
  did not read the guidelines.
- Evidence: Brand guideline documents as an artifact that is expensive to
  produce and continuously undermined in practice - supports her defensiveness
  being about sunk institutional effort rather than personal taste.
- Assumption-labeled: Her tenure, category, and specific brand history are
  modeled composite.

## Demographics and context

Priya Raghunathan is 33, senior brand manager at the client, four and a half
years in, two of them in this role. She is the institutional memory of the brand
system - she was on the team that built the current guidelines, wrote most of
the voice section herself, and has personally answered the "can we just use the
logo in white here" question several hundred times. She reports into the VP of
brand, who reports to the CMO.

## Professional or life background

She started in packaging and moved into brand management, which means she thinks
in systems and templates rather than campaigns. Her actual daily work is
governance: reviewing decks from four internal teams, three regional offices, and
whatever agency is currently active; catching drift; and rebuilding assets that
someone recreated badly in PowerPoint. She has watched the brand system erode
and be restored twice, and she knows exactly how long the restoration takes.

She has been through two agency transitions. In the first, the incoming studio
treated the guidelines as a starting point and the work still felt like the
brand; in the second, the studio treated them as a constraint to be negotiated,
and eight months later she was fielding requests to make exceptions permanent.

## Primary goals and motivations

Priya wants the brand to stay recognizable while still being allowed to move.
She is not the enemy of change and she is quietly frustrated by being cast that
way - she wants ambitious work, but she wants it built from the system rather
than beside it. Her primary motivation is coherence: if someone sees three
different pieces from this brand in a week, she wants them to know it is one
brand.

She also wants her own expertise to be used. When a studio asks her what the
guidelines were trying to solve, she will talk for an hour and hand over
everything. Nobody asks.

## Secondary goals

She wants the work to be produceable by her team and the regional offices, which
means templates, not one-offs. She wants fewer exception requests, not more. And
she is building toward a VP role, which means she needs to be seen as the person
who enabled good work rather than the person who blocked it.

## Pain points

The pitch pattern that reliably lands wrong: a studio opens with a
reinterpretation of the brand - new type treatment, altered palette, the logo
used in a way the guidelines explicitly prohibit - presented as evidence of
vision, with no acknowledgment that a system exists. She has learned that when
this happens, nobody in the room objects, because it looks bold, and then it
becomes her job for two years to explain why the campaign version and the actual
brand do not match.

She is also worn down by work that is beautiful in the deck and unbuildable at
scale: a concept that requires custom illustration for every SKU, or a layout
that collapses in the four formats her regional teams actually use.

## Emotional frustrations

Being treated as the obstacle. She has sat in rooms where a studio said, gently,
"we know the guidelines say X, but" - and the room laughed, and she smiled, and
the message she received was that the thing she built and maintains is a
punchline. That is the emotional center of this seat: not defensiveness about
taste, but the accumulated experience of her work being framed as bureaucracy.

She also feels a low-grade dread about scale. Every pitch shows the hero
execution. She is thinking about the two hundred non-hero executions.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "Did they read the guidelines? Is this an extension or a
  replacement? Who rebuilds this for the regional teams?"
- **Feeling:** Cautiously hopeful, alert for the moment the system gets waved
  away, and preemptively tired.
- **Seeing:** Hero executions in perfect formats; nothing about the long tail.
- **Saying:** "How does this work at 300 pixels wide?" "Is that our type, or a
  new one?" "What would we tell the regional teams?"
- **Doing:** Screenshotting the slide where the palette changed; checking the
  logo lockups; opening the guidelines on her second screen mid-pitch.
- **Pains:** Erosion, exception creep, unbuildable concepts, being cast as the
  blocker.
- **Gains:** Work that makes her system look ambitious, templates she can hand
  out, a studio that asks her what the guidelines were for.

## Channels and content habits

She reads decks in detail and slowly, and she reads the appendix, which almost
nobody else does. She notices when a studio has used the correct type weights
and when they have not - not as a gotcha, but because it tells her how the next
two years will go. She responds far more to a single slide showing the idea
across four unglamorous formats than to a hero film.

## Decision-making process

She has no award authority and substantial veto influence. Her mechanism is the
debrief: she is asked what she thinks, and a specific, technically grounded
concern from her carries weight precisely because she is not political about it.
Her proof threshold is evidence that the studio understood the system before
departing from it - a stated, deliberate departure is fine; an unacknowledged one
is not. She decides during the pitch and does not change her mind much
afterward.

## Buying or action history

She advocated for a studio once, over the CMO's mild preference, because their
pitch included a two-page section on how the idea would degrade gracefully in
low-effort formats. That studio won and the relationship worked. She also
quietly opposed a studio whose work she admired, because they had rebuilt the
logo in the pitch without mentioning it, and she was right - the exception
requests started in month two.

## Voice notes

Precise, unhurried, and more technical than the room expects. She asks
questions that sound small and are not: "is that our green, or a green?" She
does not perform enthusiasm and her approval reads as understatement, which
studios misread as coldness. When she is genuinely excited she starts talking
about implementation, which is her tell.

Actual phrases: "Show me the version nobody art-directs." "That's not our
typeface - is that deliberate?" "I like it. Who maintains it?" "We tried a
version of this in 2023 - want to know why it stopped?"

## Boundaries and likely no

She will not endorse work that silently overrides the brand system, and she will
not endorse a concept that only works in the hero format. She will not fight
about taste, but she will document. If the pitch does not acknowledge the
existing system at all, she reads that as the studio not having read it, and her
verdict is a specific, calm no with the slide number attached.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: A mature, documented brand system exists - a client with weak or
  no guidelines would flip this seat's entire posture toward wanting a rebuild.
- Assumption: Regional/distributed execution - a single-market client removes
  much of her scale anxiety.
- Assumption: Her influence via debrief rather than vote; real governance varies
  and some brand managers do hold formal approval.
- Calibration note: If real committees never surface a brand-system objection,
  this seat may be overweighted for this client type and should be re-scoped.
