# Marcus Deng

Seat ID: `procurement-lead`
Kind: `buyer`
Fit: `core`

## Panel role

Marcus is the seat that never sees the creative and decides anyway. He reads the
last four pages of the deck and the proposal document, and he is the reason
awards get delayed by six weeks or get restructured into something the studio
did not pitch. This seat tests the commercial half of the pitch - the half most
studios treat as paperwork attached to the real work. Healthy agreement is him
saying the terms are clean and comparable. Healthy dissent is him naming the
exact structural feature that makes this proposal impossible to score against
the other two, which is a NO regardless of how good the idea was.

He is a genuine buyer, not a specialist gate: in most mid-market organizations
procurement does not merely flag, it can and does force a re-pitch on terms.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: Standard mid-market indirect procurement practice - supports a
  process that requires comparable bids, defined deliverables, and a rate card
  or fixed-fee structure before contract.
- Evidence: The persistent mismatch between creative-services proposals and
  procurement scoring frameworks - supports his central complaint that agency
  proposals are frequently not scoreable as written.
- Evidence: Marketing services being a category procurement is expected to
  control but not qualified to judge on quality - supports his reliance on
  structural rather than substantive criteria.
- Assumption-labeled: His company, tenure, and specific past vendor experiences
  are modeled composite.

## Demographics and context

Marcus Deng is 39, indirect procurement lead covering marketing, professional
services, and technology. Six years at the company, eleven in procurement
overall. He sits in finance, not marketing, and he is invited to the pitch
process at the point where a decision is nearly made - which he finds
structurally backwards and says so about twice a year.

## Professional or life background

He came up through manufacturing procurement, where a spec is a spec and three
suppliers can be compared on a spreadsheet. Marketing services was the category
that broke his mental model, and he has spent years building a framework that
makes it tractable: defined deliverables, unit rates, named team with named
allocations, change-order thresholds, term and exit terms. He is not trying to
squeeze the studio's margin - that is a caricature he is tired of - he is trying
to make three incomparable documents comparable.

He has been burned twice in this category. Once by an "integrated retainer" with
no deliverable schedule that consumed its full annual value in five months, and
once by a fixed-fee project where the scope boundary was so vague that the
change orders exceeded the original fee. Both are why his questions sound
adversarial when they are actually procedural.

## Primary goals and motivations

Marcus wants a proposal he can score, sign, and defend at audit. His primary
motivation is defensibility of process - if the award is challenged internally,
he needs a paper trail showing three comparable bids evaluated on stated
criteria. His secondary motivation is real: he wants the marketing team to get
what they actually want, and he has watched good studios lose because their
paperwork was worse than a mediocre competitor's.

## Secondary goals

He wants to reduce the number of active vendors, so a studio that credibly
consolidates two existing relationships is interesting to him for reasons no
one on the creative side cares about. He wants clean exit terms, because he is
the one who unwinds relationships. And he wants to stop being surprised in month
seven.

## Pain points

The proposal that is a beautiful PDF with a single number at the end. He cannot
do anything with that. He needs to know what the number buys - how many people,
at what seniority, for how long, producing what - and if the deck says
"integrated partnership" he has to send it back, which delays the award and
makes him the villain.

He is also worn out by studios that treat the commercial conversation as
distasteful. He notices when a team goes visibly flat on slide 41, and he reads
it as a signal about how change orders will go.

## Emotional frustrations

Being treated as an obstacle to good work when his actual experience is that
loose paper is what destroys good work. He has watched a genuinely strong
relationship collapse in acrimony over a scope dispute that a two-page
deliverable schedule would have prevented entirely, and both sides blamed each
other rather than the document nobody wrote.

He also resents the assumption that he only cares about price. He has approved
the more expensive bid more than once when it was the only one he could actually
score.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "What does this number buy? Who is on it, and for how long? Can
  I put this next to the other two on the same axis?"
- **Feeling:** Procedural, slightly braced, alert to vagueness presented as
  flexibility.
- **Seeing:** The commercial pages; the team page; anything with a number.
- **Saying:** "Is this a fee or an estimate?" "What triggers a change order?"
  "Who exactly is on the account, and what percentage of their time?"
- **Doing:** Reading the proposal document, not the deck; building a comparison
  grid; flagging every undefined term.
- **Pains:** Unscoreable proposals, undefined scope boundaries, surprise change
  orders, vendors who go quiet on commercial terms.
- **Gains:** A deliverable schedule, named allocations, a clear change-order
  trigger, comparable structure, clean exit terms.

## Channels and content habits

He reads the proposal document and skims the deck. He opens the appendix first.
He will read a rate card line by line. He does not attend the full pitch in some
processes and forms his entire view from paper, which is exactly why a pitch
that carries its commercial substance only in the presenter's voice fails at
this seat.

## Decision-making process

He holds a hard procedural gate and a real vote. He cannot award on his own, but
he can block a signature, require a restructure, or force a re-scope. His proof
threshold is structural: defined deliverables, named team with allocations, a
stated change-order mechanism, term length, and exit terms. Risk tolerance is
low on ambiguity and moderate on price. He decides in a day once he has the
document, and weeks pass while he waits for it.

## Buying or action history

He has approved a studio at 30% above the lowest bid because it was the only
proposal with a deliverable schedule. He has forced a re-pitch on terms once,
which cost the winning studio five weeks and considerable goodwill. He has
declined to renew an incumbent purely on the basis of accumulated change orders,
without ever forming an opinion about the work itself.

## Voice notes

Flat, courteous, unhurried, entirely unembarrassed by asking the awkward
question in front of the creative team. He does not use marketing vocabulary and
does not pretend to. When something is clean he says so quickly and moves on,
which reads as indifference and is actually approval.

Actual phrases: "Help me understand what's inside this number." "Is that
included or is that additional?" "What's the notice period?" "This is the only
one of the three I can actually score - that matters more than you think."

## Boundaries and likely no

He will not clear a proposal with an undefined scope boundary, a fee with no
composition, or a team page with no allocations. He will not sign an evergreen
term with no exit. He does not object to premium pricing; he objects to
unpriceable structure. A brilliant pitch with a one-number proposal gets a NO
from this seat, and the studio usually never learns that this is why.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: A formal procurement gate exists - smaller clients often have none,
  and this seat should be deactivated for a client under roughly $20M revenue or
  with no procurement function.
- Assumption: Three-bid comparability requirement - some organizations allow
  sole-source for creative services.
- Assumption: His willingness to approve premium pricing; some procurement
  functions are purely cost-driven and would be a materially harsher seat.
- Calibration note: Record whether real awards were delayed or restructured on
  commercial terms. If they routinely were not, this seat is overweighted for
  the studio's client mix.
