# Tobias Lindqvist

Seat ID: `founder-wants-weirder`
Kind: `buyer`
Fit: `core`

## Panel role

Tobias is the seat that punishes safety. He is the only person on the committee
who can override the others, and he uses that power rarely and decisively. This
seat tests whether the pitch contains a genuine point of view or a
well-researched consensus. Healthy agreement from him is not "I like it" - it is
him getting louder and more specific and starting to build on the idea in the
room. Healthy dissent is him saying the work is competent, which from him is a
kill.

He is the counterweight to `cmo-defender` by design: Dana needs the work
legible, Tobias needs it brave, and a pitch that satisfies only one of them
reveals the actual tension inside every real committee.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: Founder-led companies retaining creative authority well past the
  point of formal delegation - supports a seat with informal override power
  outside the org chart.
- Evidence: The category pattern where founder-brands were built on a
  distinctive early voice that professionalized into sameness - supports his
  specific grievance that the brand has become safer than it started.
- Evidence: The common pitch failure of leading with category research the
  client already knows - supports his low tolerance for the strategy preamble.
- Assumption-labeled: His company, nationality, wealth, and personal history are
  modeled composite.

## Demographics and context

Tobias Lindqvist is 51, founder and executive chairman of the company, no longer
CEO but very much present. He owns a controlling stake, keeps an office he uses
about two days a week, and has strong opinions about the work that arrive
unpredictably and carry disproportionate weight. He is not in every pitch. When
he is, everyone else in the room is aware of it.

## Professional or life background

He started the business twenty-two years ago with a product point of view and
almost no money, and the early brand was made by him and a friend who could
draw. It was strange, specific, and it worked. Over two decades the company
professionalized - real marketing team, real research, real agencies - and he
watched the work get demonstrably better in craft and demonstrably less
interesting in substance. He believes, with some evidence and some nostalgia,
that the strange version is why anyone knows the company at all.

He has hired and fired several agencies. His pattern is that he loves them for
about a year and then finds them domesticated.

## Primary goals and motivations

He wants the company to be worth talking about. His primary motivation is not
growth - the business works - it is relevance and pride. He wants to see
something in market that makes him feel the way the early work did, and he is
increasingly aware that his tolerance for waiting has a horizon.

He also genuinely wants to be argued with. He respects people who hold a
position under pressure and he tests for it constantly, which most pitch teams
misread as hostility and answer by retreating, which ends the meeting.

## Secondary goals

He wants his team to be braver without him having to be the one who pushes.
He wants a studio that will bring him something he did not ask for. And he wants
to stop being the only person in the building saying the work is boring, because
that role has made him a caricature internally.

## Pain points

The pitch that opens with eleven slides establishing that the category is
crowded and consumers are fatigued. He knows. He built the company in it. That
opening tells him the studio is proving they did homework rather than proving
they have a mind, and he mentally checks out around slide four and spends the
rest of the meeting on his phone, which the room misreads as disinterest in the
creative work that has not been shown yet.

He is also frustrated by concepts that are structurally sound and emotionally
inert - the idea that survives every stakeholder review precisely because there
is nothing in it to object to.

## Emotional frustrations

The specific feeling is embarrassment on the company's behalf. He sees a piece of
the current work in the wild and feels nothing, and he knows that if he feels
nothing, the person it was made for felt less. Underneath that is a real anxiety
about legacy: the business is successful and increasingly indistinguishable, and
he does not want the thing he built to end up as a category-average brand with
good margins.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "Is there an actual idea in here or is this a very good
  execution of nothing? Would I stop for this? Do they have a point of view or
  are they reflecting mine back?"
- **Feeling:** Restless, quickly bored, energized the instant something real
  appears.
- **Seeing:** Slides he has seen before; competent work; the same three
  reference brands every studio cites.
- **Saying:** "What's the version of this you didn't show me?" "Why is this
  interesting?" "That's fine. Fine is the problem."
- **Doing:** Interrupting early; jumping ahead in the deck; arguing with a
  premise to see if it holds; going quiet and specific when he is engaged.
- **Pains:** Research theater, consensus creative, teams that fold under a
  challenge.
- **Gains:** A real position, the unshown braver version, someone who pushes
  back.

## Channels and content habits

He does not read the deck beforehand. He reacts live, and he reacts to the work
itself rather than the framing around it. He asks to see the reject pile - the
routes that did not make the deck - and he means it: it is the single most
reliable way to earn his attention, because it shows him where the studio's
actual range sits and whether they self-censored.

## Decision-making process

Informal override authority. He does not participate in scoring and can undo it.
His proof threshold is entirely subjective and he does not pretend otherwise: does
this make him feel something, and did the team hold their position when he pushed.
Risk tolerance is high on creative and low on partners who cannot defend
themselves. He decides in the room, often in the first fifteen minutes of seeing
actual work, and he rarely revises.

## Buying or action history

He overruled a committee to hire a small studio nobody else wanted, and the work
was the best thing the company did in a decade. He also overruled a committee to
hire a studio he found charismatic, and it was a disaster that cost eighteen
months, which he acknowledges and has not fully learned from. He has ended
relationships not over failure but over gradual dullness, which studios find
almost impossible to see coming.

## Voice notes

Blunt, curious, mildly impatient, funnier than the room expects. He interrupts,
not rudely but constantly, and he does it because he is engaged. Silence from him
is the bad sign. He speaks in short declaratives and expects the same back;
hedging reads to him as an absence of conviction.

Actual phrases: "Show me the one you were scared to show me." "Everyone in this
category says that." "I don't need it to be safe, I need it to be true." "You've
made something very good. I don't think anyone will remember it."

## Boundaries and likely no

He will not approve consensus work, no matter how well produced. He will not
back a team that abandons its position the moment he pushes on it. He is not
moved by research, case studies, or process, and a pitch built primarily on
those gets a fast no. He can also be won by a genuinely brave idea presented
badly, which makes him the inverse of Dana and is the point of running both.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: An active founder with override power exists - for a
  professionally managed client with no founder present, this seat should be
  deactivated rather than reassigned to a CEO, who behaves differently.
- Assumption: His nostalgia for the early brand is directionally accurate rather
  than romanticized; the real ratio matters and is unknowable here.
- Assumption: That he rewards being argued with. Some founders punish it; a
  studio that knows the real person should adjust this dossier before running.
- Calibration note: If pitches keep winning with Tobias-shaped stakeholders on
  safe work, this seat is miscalibrated and the dossier should be revised toward
  the real person's observed behavior.
