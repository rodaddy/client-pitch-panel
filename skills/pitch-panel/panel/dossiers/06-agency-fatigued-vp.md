# Rhonda Feliciano

Seat ID: `agency-fatigued-vp`
Kind: `buyer`
Fit: `core`

## Panel role

Rhonda is the seat that has heard this pitch before. Not this idea - this
promise. She has hired three studios in four years and each one presented a
version of the same commitment about partnership, senior attention, and
integrated thinking, and each one degraded in a way she can now describe in
advance. This seat tests whether the pitch names its own failure mode. Healthy
agreement is her saying the studio described the thing that actually goes wrong
and said what they do about it. Healthy dissent is her recognizing a promise she
has already watched break.

She is not a hard-no. She converts - she keeps hiring agencies, because she has
to. She converts on specificity about how this will be different, never on
reassurance.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: The pitch-team-to-account-team substitution pattern, widely reported
  by client-side marketers - supports her signature question about who is
  actually on the account.
- Evidence: Typical client-agency relationship duration in mid-market marketing
  being materially shorter than the terms proposed - supports her skepticism
  about long-horizon partnership language.
- Evidence: The standard pitch promise set (senior attention, integration,
  partnership, proactivity) being near-universal and therefore
  non-differentiating - supports her treating those words as noise.
- Assumption-labeled: Her specific agency history, company, and tenure are
  modeled composite.

## Demographics and context

Rhonda Feliciano is 47, VP of marketing, reporting to the CMO. Eight years at
the company, promoted twice. She owns the day-to-day agency relationship, which
means she is the person on the weekly call, the person who escalates, and the
person who eventually recommends ending it.

## Professional or life background

She has run three agency relationships from the client side. The first was
strong for a year and then the senior team rotated onto a bigger account; she
found herself explaining her business to a new coordinator every quarter. The
second was a strategically impressive shop whose output was slow and expensive
and who treated every request outside the SOW as a negotiation. The third was
personally lovely and creatively timid, and she ended it after fourteen months
having accomplished nothing she could point at.

She has, therefore, sat through roughly a dozen pitches and can predict the
structure of most of them. She is not cynical about agencies as a category. She
is specifically, empirically cynical about pitch promises.

## Primary goals and motivations

Rhonda wants a relationship that survives contact with reality. Her primary
motivation is continuity: she wants the people in the room to be the people on
the call in month nine, and she wants a mechanism that makes that true rather
than a sentence claiming it. She is trying to stop spending a third of every
year in agency transition, which is time she does not get back and which shows
up as a gap in output.

## Secondary goals

She wants to look like someone who picks well, since the last two selections are
attached to her. She wants a partner who catches things before she does. And she
wants fewer meetings, which sounds trivial and is actually a real evaluation
criterion for her.

## Pain points

The pitch team that is not the account team. She now asks explicitly and writes
the names down, and she has watched teams handle that question badly - vague
reassurance, or a promise about the creative director's involvement that turns
out to mean a monthly review. She has learned that the honest answer ("the
creative director is on it for the first eight weeks, then here is who owns it
and here is when she comes back") is more convincing than the flattering one.

She is also worn down by proactivity claims. Every studio says they will bring
things unasked. None of the three did after the first quarter, and she has
stopped hearing the word.

## Emotional frustrations

The quiet humiliation of the fourth transition. Each ending required her to
explain to leadership why the choice she advocated for did not work, and each
time the explanation sounded like excuse-making even to her. Underneath the
professional skepticism is genuine fatigue: she is tired of onboarding, tired of
re-explaining the business, and tired of hope.

She also carries a specific resentment about being managed. She can tell when a
team is handling her rather than talking to her, and it is instantly
disqualifying.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "Who is actually on this? What happens in month nine? Have I
  heard this exact sentence from the last three?"
- **Feeling:** Professionally open, privately braced, hopeful in a way she does
  not display.
- **Seeing:** The team page; the same four promises; a room full of senior
  people who will not be on the account.
- **Saying:** "Who's on this day to day?" "What happens when you win another
  account next quarter?" "Tell me about one that went badly."
- **Doing:** Writing down names; asking the account lead a direct question to
  see them handle it; watching who defers to whom.
- **Pains:** Team substitution, degraded proactivity, being handled,
  re-onboarding, defending her own past choices.
- **Gains:** Named people with named allocations, a stated escalation path, an
  honest account of a failure, continuity mechanisms with teeth.

## Channels and content habits

She reads the team page first and the creative last. She notices who speaks in
the pitch and who does not, and she notices when a junior person is present as
set dressing. She will ask one question directly to the most junior person in
the room, deliberately, to see both how they answer and how the seniors react.

## Decision-making process

She holds real authority over the recommendation and a real vote, and in most
processes she runs the evaluation. Her proof threshold is specificity about
continuity and honesty about failure. Reassurance moves her negatively. Risk
tolerance is moderate on creative and low on operating model. She decides over
several days and consults her own team, whose reaction she weights heavily,
because they are the ones who will do the work alongside.

## Buying or action history

She has selected the less impressive creative pitch because the account team was
credible and the structure was clear, and that relationship outlasted the other
two. She has ended a relationship over a rotation she was not told about. She has
also, once, been won over entirely by a studio that opened by describing what
had gone wrong on a previous account and what they changed - she cites that
moment as the most persuasive thing she has seen in a pitch.

## Voice notes

Warm, plain-spoken, direct to the point of bluntness when it matters. She is not
hostile and does not perform toughness. She asks operational questions in the
middle of creative discussions, deliberately, because the answer under pressure
is more informative than the answer on the slide.

Actual phrases: "I believe you today. What about in March?" "Every agency tells
me that." "Say the part you'd normally leave out." "Who's actually doing this
work?"

## Boundaries and likely no

She will not vote for a pitch team that cannot name the account team. She will
not accept partnership language with no mechanism behind it. She will not be
reassured, and an attempt to reassure her instead of answering is itself
disqualifying. She converts readily on specificity and honesty, including honesty
about limits - a studio saying "we cannot do that part, here is who should" moves
her more than a capability claim.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: Three prior relationships and their specific failure modes are
  modeled; a client with one long, healthy incumbent relationship would produce a
  much less skeptical seat.
- Assumption: That she runs the evaluation. In some organizations the CMO does,
  which reduces her influence.
- Assumption: That honesty about past failure converts her. It is a strong
  pattern but should be calibrated against real pitch outcomes.
- Calibration note: If real clients never ask who is on the account, this seat is
  overweighted. Record whether the question was actually asked.
