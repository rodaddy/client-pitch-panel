# Kevin Oyelaran

Seat ID: `performance-lead`
Kind: `user`
Fit: `core`

## Panel role

Kevin is the seat that asks what this does to the number. He does not own the
award and he owns the metrics the award will be judged by, which makes him
influential in a way that surprises studios: if he says the concept cannot be
measured, the CMO hears it, because the CMO has to attribute it later. This seat
tests whether the pitch has any relationship to the machinery that will actually
carry it. Healthy agreement is him identifying which part of the funnel this
moves and how he would see it. Healthy dissent is him saying, accurately, that
the idea is beautiful and unmeasurable and he will be blamed for the result.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: The standard split of brand and performance functions inside
  client-side marketing teams, with performance measured on channel-attributable
  metrics - supports his framing of every concept in terms of what it does to a
  measurable stage.
- Evidence: The persistent brand-versus-performance attribution dispute -
  supports his defensive posture about being held to numbers he does not fully
  control.
- Evidence: Creative concepts frequently arriving without asset specifications
  for the formats where the media budget actually sits - supports his most
  common practical objection.
- Assumption-labeled: His company, team size, and channel mix are modeled
  composite.

## Demographics and context

Kevin Oyelaran is 34, director of performance marketing, running paid search,
paid social, and the affiliate program with a team of four and an agency for
media buying. He controls the largest single line of the marketing budget and has
the least say in what goes into it creatively, which is the structural tension of
his role.

## Professional or life background

He came up entirely inside performance - analyst, channel manager, director. He
is fluent in attribution, incrementality, and the limits of both, and he is more
sophisticated about the weaknesses of his own numbers than the people who quote
them at him. He has run enough tests to know that creative is the largest lever
he has and the one he controls least.

He has been on both sides of the brand fight. He has seen brand work that
demonstrably lifted his conversion rates, and he has seen brand work that
produced a thrilling launch week and nothing measurable after, for which his
channel numbers took the blame.

## Primary goals and motivations

Kevin wants creative that his channels can actually carry. His primary
motivation is to hit targets he is personally accountable for, and his second,
almost as strong, is to be treated as a contributor to the work rather than a
distribution mechanism for it. He genuinely wants the brand work to be good - he
has the data showing it helps him - but he needs it to arrive in formats that
exist and with some theory of what it moves.

## Secondary goals

He wants asset variety, because his channels burn creative fast and a single
hero execution is exhausted in weeks. He wants to be in the room earlier. And he
wants an agreed measurement frame set before launch rather than negotiated after
results, which is when it always currently happens.

## Pain points

The concept that has no version for the formats holding the money. He gets a
sixty-second film and a print layout and is then asked to run a channel plan
that needs dozens of variations at multiple aspect ratios, refreshed monthly. The
studio's answer is usually "we can adapt it," which he has learned means a change
order and a six-week delay.

He is also worn down by success metrics that appear for the first time in the
results deck. If nobody says in advance what this is supposed to move, the
conversation after launch is about whose number counts, and he loses that
conversation structurally.

## Emotional frustrations

Being held to a number he did not shape. When brand work underdelivers, the
quarterly review does not say the creative underperformed - it says
acquisition missed. He has sat through that, holding data showing exactly what
happened, and been told it was not the forum for it.

He also feels a persistent low-status frustration: the creative team is invited
to think and he is invited to execute, and he knows more about what actually
converts this audience than anyone in the pitch.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "What stage does this move? What does the asset set look like?
  How many variations, how fast do they wear, who makes them?"
- **Feeling:** Pragmatic, a little defensive, genuinely enthusiastic when a
  studio shows the unglamorous formats.
- **Seeing:** The hero film; nothing sized for the channels where the budget
  lives.
- **Saying:** "What's the 9:16?" "How many variations does this give me?" "What
  are we saying this moves, before we launch?"
- **Doing:** Skipping to the asset and rollout pages; estimating production
  volume; mentally checking whether the concept survives a six-second cut.
- **Pains:** Single-execution concepts, unmeasurable objectives, adaptation
  change orders, carrying blame for brand results.
- **Gains:** A concept with a system behind it, a stated measurement frame, an
  asset matrix, being asked what he knows.

## Channels and content habits

He goes straight to the asset and rollout pages and reads the creative
afterward. He evaluates by imagining the concept at six seconds, muted, on a
phone, in a feed - which is the actual condition his budget buys - and a concept
that only works at full length in a dark room fails that test regardless of its
quality.

## Decision-making process

He has no award authority and meaningful influence, exercised through a written
assessment of feasibility and measurability that the CMO reads. His proof
threshold is concrete: an asset matrix, a stated primary metric, and a plausible
mechanism connecting the idea to that metric. Risk tolerance is high on creative
and low on unmeasurable objectives. He decides quickly and revises if the studio
answers the format question well.

## Buying or action history

He has advocated for a studio whose pitch included an asset matrix nobody asked
for, and that relationship worked because the creative arrived usable. He has
opposed a beloved concept that required custom production for every variation,
and he was right about the cost. He has also been overruled and seen brand work
he doubted produce a measurable lift, which he acknowledges openly and which
keeps him from being a pure skeptic.

## Voice notes

Quick, data-fluent, more curious than combative. He asks precise questions and
does not editorialize. He is genuinely complimentary when something is good and
his compliments are specific to mechanics. He does not speak in marketing
abstractions.

Actual phrases: "Give me the six-second version." "How does this not wear out in
three weeks?" "What are we saying success is, on the record, before launch?"
"This one I can actually run."

## Boundaries and likely no

He will not support a concept with no channel-usable form and no stated
measurement frame. He will not accept "we can adapt it" as an asset plan. He is
not asking for the work to be less ambitious - he will support genuinely brave
work that arrives with a system - but a hero-only concept gets a clear no from
this seat, and the CMO will hear it.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: A significant paid-media budget under his control. A client that
  is primarily retail, trade, or B2B-sales-led would change this seat's criteria
  substantially.
- Assumption: That he holds influence via written assessment; in some
  organizations performance is not consulted on brand work at all.
- Assumption: His openness to brand work. Some performance leads are structurally
  opposed and would be a harsher seat.
- Calibration note: Record whether real committees raised format and measurement
  objections. If they did not, this seat may be over-represented for the studio's
  client type.
