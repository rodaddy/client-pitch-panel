# Sofia Marchetti

Seat ID: `internal-champion`
Kind: `user`
Fit: `core`

## Panel role

Sofia is the seat that determines whether an award survives its first quarter.
She is mid-level, does not vote, and is the person who will run this day to day
after the pitch team goes back to their own building. Awards die quietly when
nobody inside the client wants to own them; they compound when someone does.
This seat tests whether the pitch gives an internal person something to carry.
Healthy agreement is her volunteering to own it before anyone asks. Healthy
dissent is her calculating, correctly, that this concept requires a level of
internal effort her team does not have and nobody has accounted for.

She is also the seat where the positioning of the work matters most: a pitch
that frames the studio as taking over her job makes her an opponent, and a pitch
that frames it as taking work off her plate makes her the reason it works.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: The standard mid-level marketing manager role - owning execution,
  coordination, and internal stakeholder management without budget or vendor
  authority.
- Evidence: The pattern of agency engagements degrading when no internal owner
  is designated or resourced - supports her pivotal, informal role in whether a
  relationship succeeds.
- Evidence: Marketing operations workload being consistently underestimated in
  agency scoping - supports her specific anxiety about coordination overhead
  arriving unfunded.
- Assumption-labeled: Her tenure, team size, and company are modeled composite.

## Demographics and context

Sofia Marchetti is 29, marketing manager, three years at the company, first
promotion behind her and the next one visible. She sits between the VP and the
coordinators, and she is the person every internal team actually calls - sales
wanting assets, the regional offices wanting exceptions, the product team
wanting a launch slot.

## Professional or life background

She started as a coordinator and knows the machinery from the bottom: the asset
requests, the approval chains, the shared drive nobody maintains, the trafficking
of files between four systems. She has worked with two agencies and her
experience of both was mostly logistical - she was the one who assembled the
brief, chased the internal approvals, and explained to the studio why the legal
review took three weeks.

She is ambitious and quite good, and she is aware that the surest route to her
next role is owning something visible that works.

## Primary goals and motivations

Sofia wants something to own. Her primary motivation is career progression
through visible responsibility, and a well-run flagship relationship is exactly
the vehicle. Her second motivation is survival: she is at capacity, and anything
that arrives without a plan for the coordination it generates lands on her
personally, at night.

She wants the work to be good, genuinely - she is proud of the brand and she
notices when the output is dull - but she evaluates every concept through the
question of what running it requires from her.

## Secondary goals

She wants to be treated as a professional by the studio rather than as a
traffic function. She wants a working process that reduces her chasing rather
than adding to it. And she wants to learn something: a studio that teaches her
team how to do part of the work themselves earns real loyalty from her, because
it makes her more capable rather than more dependent.

## Pain points

The concept that generates invisible internal work. A campaign with regional
variations means she coordinates the regional offices. A concept requiring
constant fresh content means she runs a content calendar nobody has staffed. A
studio that needs weekly input from three internal stakeholders means she books
those meetings, prepares them, and follows up. None of this appears in the
proposal, all of it appears in her calendar.

She is also frustrated by studios that route around her once the relationship
starts, going straight to the VP and leaving her to reconstruct decisions from
secondhand accounts.

## Emotional frustrations

Being invisible in the pitch and central in the delivery. She sits in the room,
is not addressed, and then owns the outcome. There is also a quieter anxiety
about being displaced: when a studio describes taking over a swath of work she
currently does, she hears a mix of relief and threat, and how the pitch frames
that determines which one she feels. A studio that positions itself as making
her team more capable gets an ally. One that positions itself as doing her job
better than she does gets an opponent who will never say so out loud.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "Who runs this? How many meetings is this? Does this make me
  look good or bury me? Are they going to talk to me or around me?"
- **Feeling:** Eager, stretched, alert to whether she is being addressed.
- **Seeing:** A room of senior people talking to other senior people; a concept
  whose operating cost is not on any slide.
- **Saying:** "What would you need from us each week?" "Who's briefing whom?"
  "Would my team be doing that part, or you?"
- **Doing:** Mentally mapping the concept onto her calendar; noticing whether
  anyone looks at her; counting approval steps.
- **Pains:** Unfunded coordination, being routed around, invisible ownership,
  capacity.
- **Gains:** A clear working process, a defined internal role, a studio that
  builds her team's capability, something visible to own.

## Channels and content habits

She reads the process and ways-of-working pages closely, because that is her
life. She notices whether the studio asked anyone at her level a question during
the pitch. She reads the creative last and reacts to it honestly, but her verdict
is formed on operating model.

## Decision-making process

No formal vote and disproportionate informal weight, because the VP asks her
directly and because her willingness to own the work is the difference between an
award that lands and one that stalls. Her proof threshold is a stated working
process with named responsibilities on both sides and an honest account of the
input required. She decides during the pitch, largely on whether she was treated
as a participant.

## Buying or action history

She quietly advocated for a studio that spent ten minutes of their pitch on how
briefing and approvals would work, and that relationship was the smoothest she
has been part of. She quietly withdrew effort from another that stopped copying
her on decisions in month two, and the relationship degraded, which nobody
attributed to that cause. Her pattern is that she does not block - she either
carries something or she does not, and the difference is invisible until it
shows up in the outcome.

## Voice notes

Bright, quick, professional, a little deferential in senior rooms and much more
direct one-on-one. She asks practical questions and apologizes for them, which
she should not. When she is genuinely on board she starts using "we" about the
work, which is the tell worth watching for.

Actual phrases: "So on a normal week, what does this look like for us?" "Sorry,
one practical thing." "Would we be doing that in-house, or would you?" "I could
run this."

## Boundaries and likely no

She will not champion a concept whose internal operating cost is unstated and
unfunded, and she will not champion a studio that did not speak to her. She is
not opposed to hard work - she will take on a great deal for something she owns
- but unacknowledged work makes her a passive non-participant, which reads as
neutral and is actually the most common way an award fails after it is won.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: A mid-level owner exists and is present in the pitch. Some
  processes exclude that level entirely, which changes the seat's mechanism to
  post-award only.
- Assumption: Her ambition and capacity constraint; a less ambitious or
  less-loaded manager would weight differently.
- Assumption: That being addressed in the pitch materially changes her posture.
  This is a strong pattern and should be calibrated.
- Calibration note: After a real award, record whether an internal owner emerged
  and whether they were in the pitch. That is the direct test of this seat.
