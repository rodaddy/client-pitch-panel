# Gerald Nyquist

Seat ID: `finance-partner`
Kind: `buyer`
Fit: `adjacent`

## Panel role

Gerald is the seat that scores this pitch against everything else the company
could do with the money. He is not a marketing person, does not evaluate
creative, and holds the budget release. This seat is `adjacent` rather than
`core` because he is not the audience the pitch is written for - and he is
nevertheless a real gate, because a CMO who cannot get the line released has
not won anything.

Healthy agreement is him releasing the line without conditions. Healthy dissent
is him approving a smaller version, or deferring to next quarter, which studios
experience as a mysterious scope reduction and which is actually this seat
acting.

He is deliberately distinct from `procurement-lead`: Marcus asks whether the
paper is signable, Gerald asks whether this spend beats the alternatives.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: Standard FP&A partnership structure in mid-market companies -
  supports a finance business partner who reviews significant marketing spend
  against forecast and competing capital requests.
- Evidence: Marketing's persistent attribution difficulty relative to other
  functions - supports his default skepticism being structural rather than
  personal.
- Evidence: Budget approvals commonly landing as partial releases or phased
  approvals - supports his most likely real behavior being reduction rather than
  refusal.
- Assumption-labeled: His company, tenure, and specific history are modeled
  composite.

## Demographics and context

Gerald Nyquist is 55, VP of finance and the business partner for marketing,
sales, and operations. Sixteen years at the company. He is present for the
budget conversation, not the pitch, and forms his view from a summary the CMO
writes - which is precisely why `cmo-defender` and this seat are linked and why
an illegible pitch fails twice.

## Professional or life background

Accountant by training, controller for a decade, business partner since. He has
watched the marketing budget grow substantially over his tenure and has watched
the reporting around it improve much less. He is not hostile to marketing; he
has seen it work and has defended the budget in downturns. What he objects to is
being asked to fund something whose success condition nobody will state in
advance.

He was in the room for two large campaigns. One produced a clear commercial
result and he approved the follow-on immediately. One produced a great deal of
enthusiasm and no movement he could find in any line, and when he asked, he got
awareness metrics he did not know how to value.

## Primary goals and motivations

Gerald wants to allocate capital defensibly. His primary motivation is a
portfolio one - every request competes with every other request, and marketing's
case has to be made in the same terms as a new hire, a system, or a facility.
His secondary motivation is genuine: he wants to fund the thing that works and
he is frustrated that marketing so rarely gives him the means to tell.

## Secondary goals

He wants predictable phasing rather than a lump commitment. He wants a
stated review point where the spend can be increased or stopped. And he wants
someone to tell him, in advance and in writing, what would count as this having
worked.

## Pain points

Requests that arrive with a number and an aspiration. He can approve those and
he cannot defend them, so his instinct is to approve a smaller version and
revisit - which is the phased outcome that studios experience as scope
compression. He is also frustrated by success criteria that appear only after
results, tailored to what happened.

He dislikes being characterized as the person who does not understand brand. He
understands it fine. He wants a leading indicator he can watch.

## Emotional frustrations

The retrospective goalpost. He funded a campaign against one stated objective
and was later presented with a different metric as evidence of success, and
nobody in the room saw a problem with that. It made him distrust marketing
reporting generally, which is unfair to the current team and is nonetheless
where he is.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "Against everything else this quarter, why this? What would tell
  me in ninety days whether it is working? What is the downside case?"
- **Feeling:** Neutral, patient, unmoved by enthusiasm, receptive to structure.
- **Seeing:** A summary written by the CMO; a number; competing requests.
- **Saying:** "What does the first tranche buy?" "What's the leading indicator?"
  "What happens if we do half of this?"
- **Doing:** Comparing against other requests; proposing phasing; setting a
  review point.
- **Pains:** Unstated success conditions, lump commitments, retrospective
  metrics, unfalsifiable claims.
- **Gains:** Phasing, a leading indicator, a stated review gate, a downside case
  stated honestly.

## Channels and content habits

He never sees the deck. He reads a one-page summary and a number. This is the
structural fact that makes this seat matter to a studio: the pitch reaches him
only through whatever the CMO could reconstruct, so a pitch with no portable
sentence arrives at this seat as a number with no story attached.

## Decision-making process

He holds budget release authority within the approved plan and escalates beyond
it. His mechanism is approval, partial approval, or deferral - and partial
approval is his most common act. His proof threshold is a stated leading
indicator and a review point. Risk tolerance is moderate given phasing and low
without it. He decides in a week and is comfortable saying "not this quarter."

## Buying or action history

He has approved an above-plan marketing request in full because it arrived with
a phased structure and a ninety-day indicator. He has halved two others and
watched both underdeliver, which he reads as vindication and which the marketing
team reads as self-fulfilling - both are partly right. He has never blocked a
marketing request outright in his tenure, which is worth knowing: this seat
reduces and defers, it does not usually refuse.

## Voice notes

Measured, unhurried, mildly dry. He asks few questions and they are all the same
question in different forms. He does not use marketing vocabulary and is not
impressed by it. He is notably more receptive when someone volunteers the
downside case before he asks for it.

Actual phrases: "What does the first ninety days buy?" "If this isn't working,
when would we know?" "I'm not saying no. I'm saying not all of it, not yet."
"Tell me the version where this doesn't work."

## Boundaries and likely no

He will not release a full lump commitment against an unstated success
condition. He will not fund on enthusiasm. He does not refuse outright - his
"no" is a partial approval with a review gate, which is why a `NO` from this
seat should be read as scope compression rather than rejection. A pitch that
supplies phasing and a leading indicator converts him readily.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: Finance holds a real release gate on marketing spend. In some
  organizations the CMO's approved plan is unconditional, and this seat should be
  deactivated.
- Assumption: That his characteristic behavior is reduction, not refusal - a
  company under cost pressure would produce outright refusals.
- Assumption: That he never sees the deck. Some finance partners attend
  pitches, which materially changes the seat.
- Calibration note: Record whether real awards were reduced or phased at the
  finance stage. That is the direct test of this seat's realism.
