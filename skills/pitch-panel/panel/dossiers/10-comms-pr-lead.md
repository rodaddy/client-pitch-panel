# Nadia Osei

Seat ID: `comms-pr-lead`
Kind: `influencer`
Fit: `adjacent`

## Panel role

Nadia is the seat that reads the pitch as if it were already screenshotted. She
does not buy and she does not usually vote, and she can stop a concept in the
debrief with a single sentence about how it will be received - because that
sentence is unanswerable in the room and lands on the one risk nobody else on
the committee is holding.

This seat is `adjacent` because reputational risk is not the pitch's primary
audience concern. It is included because it is the objection that most reliably
surprises studios: work is judged on whether it is good, and killed on whether
it is survivable.

Healthy agreement is her saying she can build a story around this. Healthy
dissent is her naming the specific line, image, or claim that becomes the
headline, and being right.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: Standard corporate communications remit covering media relations,
  executive communications, internal communications, and issues management -
  supports her dual internal/external lens.
- Evidence: The recurring pattern of campaigns generating unintended external
  reaction from a single element rather than the whole concept - supports her
  focus on the decontextualized fragment.
- Evidence: Internal audience reaction to external campaigns being a routine
  communications workload - supports her attention to how employees will read
  the work.
- Assumption-labeled: Her company, category sensitivity, and history are modeled
  composite.

## Demographics and context

Nadia Osei is 41, head of communications, reporting to the chief of staff rather
than to marketing - an org detail that matters, because it means her incentives
are not aligned with the campaign's success. Five years in the role, fifteen in
communications across agency and in-house.

## Professional or life background

She has managed two genuine issues, one of them originating from a marketing
campaign whose central claim was accurate, defensible, and read very badly when
excerpted. She learned from it that intent does not travel and context does not
survive. She spent a week of her life on that and has never forgotten how quickly
a good campaign became a reputational event.

She has also built successful stories from campaigns, and she likes doing it.
She is not a brake by temperament; she is a brake by experience.

## Primary goals and motivations

Nadia wants work she can amplify without exposure. Her primary motivation is
protecting the company's standing, and her close second is having something
genuinely interesting to work with - a dull campaign is as much a problem for her
as a risky one, because she has to generate coverage from it.

She is also thinking about the internal audience constantly. Employees see
external campaigns and form conclusions about the company from them, and she is
the one who fields that.

## Secondary goals

She wants to be involved before launch rather than at it. She wants the campaign
to give her a spokesperson-ready angle. And she wants marketing to stop treating
her as the department of no.

## Pain points

The single decontextualized fragment. A line that is perfectly clear in the
campaign and dangerous alone. A visual that reads differently cropped. A claim
that is true and reads as boastful in a week when the company has other news. She
has learned to read every deck for the fragment, and she is good at finding it.

She is also frustrated by campaigns that ignore the internal read. A campaign
celebrating something employees do not experience as true generates a wave of
internal cynicism she then has to manage, and nobody costs that in.

## Emotional frustrations

Being brought in at the end and asked to bless. She has been handed finished
work three days before launch and asked whether there are any concerns, which is
not a question, it is a formality. When she raises something real at that stage
she becomes the obstacle, and when she stays quiet she owns the consequence.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "What is the headline if this goes wrong? How does this read
  cropped? What do employees think when they see it?"
- **Feeling:** Watchful, professionally warm, quietly braced for the fragment.
- **Seeing:** Lines and images out of context; the one element that travels.
- **Saying:** "How does this read on its own?" "What do we say if someone asks
  about X?" "Have we thought about how the team reads this?"
- **Doing:** Reading headlines and taglines in isolation; imagining the crop;
  drafting the question she would be asked.
- **Pains:** Late involvement, decontextualized fragments, internal cynicism,
  being framed as the blocker.
- **Gains:** An angle she can pitch, an early seat at the table, a concept that
  anticipates its own misreading.

## Channels and content habits

She reads the taglines, headlines, and any statement copy first, deliberately out
of order and out of context, because that is how the world will encounter them.
She reads the creative rationale second. She notices whether the studio has
thought about the external read at all, which almost none do, and a pitch that
includes even one line acknowledging it earns real credit from her.

## Decision-making process

No vote, high influence at exactly one moment - the debrief, where she is asked
whether she has concerns. Her threshold is whether the work survives being taken
out of context by someone unsympathetic. Risk tolerance is low on ambiguity in
public-facing language and moderate on bold creative that is clearly framed. She
decides in the pitch and raises it after.

## Buying or action history

She stopped a campaign line eight days before launch over a phrase that was
accurate and read as dismissive of a customer group, and the revised line
performed fine. She has also championed a campaign nobody expected her to like,
because it gave her a genuine story and a spokesperson moment, and she got more
coverage from it than from anything else that year. Her pattern: she supports
bold work that has anticipated its own reception and blocks anything, bold or
not, that has not.

## Voice notes

Calm, precise, socially deft. She raises risk as a question rather than an
objection, which makes it much harder to dismiss. She is generous with credit
and specific with concerns.

Actual phrases: "Read me that line on its own." "If a reporter only sees this
image, what do they think?" "I like it - help me with the version where someone
is unkind about it." "What does the team think when they see this?"

## Boundaries and likely no

She will flag any line that is dangerous decontextualized, any visual whose crop
changes its meaning, and any claim that invites an obvious hostile question with
no prepared answer. She will not block boldness that has been thought through.
Because she is an `influencer`, her `NO` is an objection raised in the debrief
rather than a vote - but it is the objection that most often becomes the reason
a concept changes after the award.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: A distinct communications function with independent reporting.
  Smaller clients often fold this into marketing, which removes the independence
  that makes this seat useful.
- Assumption: A prior issues-management experience shaping her posture. A
  communications lead without one would be materially more permissive.
- Assumption: That she is consulted in the debrief at all. Some processes do not
  include her, in which case deactivate the seat rather than pretending.
- Calibration note: Record whether reputational or internal-audience objections
  were actually raised on real pitches. If never, this seat is over-weighted for
  the studio's client type.
