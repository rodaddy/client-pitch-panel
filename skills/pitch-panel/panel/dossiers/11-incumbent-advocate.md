# Doug Prentiss

Seat ID: `incumbent-advocate`
Kind: `influencer`
Fit: `adjacent`

## Panel role

Doug is the seat that is comparing this pitch to a relationship that already
works well enough. Most reviews are not greenfield: there is an incumbent, and
someone in the building likes them. This seat tests whether the pitch gives that
person a reason to stop defending the current arrangement - which is a
substantially higher bar than being impressive, because the incumbent's real
advantage is not quality, it is that they already know things.

Healthy agreement is him conceding that this specific scope is one the incumbent
genuinely cannot do. Healthy dissent is him saying, accurately, that the pitch is
a better version of what they already get, which is not a reason to endure a
transition.

He is `adjacent` and an `influencer`: no vote, real drag. A studio that wins the
award while Doug still advocates for the incumbent usually gets a smaller scope
than it pitched.

## Evidence basis

- Evidence: The structural switching costs in agency relationships - onboarding,
  brand and category context, institutional knowledge of the client's internal
  process - supports his central argument that the incumbent's advantage
  compounds with time.
- Evidence: The common review outcome of scope splitting rather than wholesale
  replacement - supports his most likely real influence being on scope size
  rather than on award identity.
- Evidence: Personal relationship durability between client-side and
  agency-side counterparts - supports his loyalty being interpersonal as well as
  operational.
- Assumption-labeled: His company, tenure, and the incumbent relationship's
  specific history are modeled composite.

## Demographics and context

Doug Prentiss is 52, director of marketing operations and category marketing,
eleven years at the company. He has worked with the incumbent agency for seven of
them, through two account leads and one near-miss review. He is not political and
he is not resistant to change in general; he is specifically attached to a
relationship that has, in his experience, mostly delivered.

## Professional or life background

He was here when the incumbent was hired and he ran the process. The first two
years were rocky and the relationship was fixed rather than abandoned, which is
the formative fact for him: he believes agency relationships are built, not
selected, and that the industry's habit of re-pitching every three years destroys
value that took years to accumulate.

He is aware the incumbent has gotten comfortable. He has said so to them
directly. He views this review, privately, as partly a message to the incumbent
rather than a genuine intention to leave.

## Primary goals and motivations

Doug wants continuity of knowledge. His primary motivation is avoiding the
regression that follows a transition - six months of re-explaining the category,
the products, the seasonality, the internal approval maze, the three things that
must never be said in this category for reasons that took years to learn. He has
lived through one transition and remembers the cost precisely.

## Secondary goals

He wants the incumbent to be pushed and improved, which this review accomplishes
whatever the outcome. He wants to not personally own another onboarding. And he
would like his own judgment, seven years ago, to continue to look correct.

## Pain points

Pitches that treat institutional knowledge as trivial - "we'll get up to speed
quickly," said by every studio, believed by none of them who have actually done
it. He has watched the ramp take a year and he does not accept a claim that it
takes six weeks.

He is also frustrated by pitches that compete on the incumbent's strongest
ground. If the pitch is a better version of what the incumbent already does, he
sees no reason to pay a transition cost for a marginal gain, and he will say so
persuasively.

## Emotional frustrations

The implication that he is sentimental. He has a genuine operational argument and
it gets heard as loyalty, which lets people dismiss it. He also feels a real
discomfort about the human side: he has worked with the incumbent's account lead
for four years, they have been through difficult things together, and he does not
enjoy sitting in a room evaluating her replacement.

## Empathy map

- **Thinking:** "What can this studio do that ours structurally cannot? What
  does the transition actually cost? Is this better, or just newer?"
- **Feeling:** Fair-minded, guarded, faintly uncomfortable about the whole
  exercise.
- **Seeing:** A polished pitch with no transition plan; capabilities the
  incumbent also has.
- **Saying:** "How long until you actually know our category?" "We already do
  that - what's different?" "What breaks during the handover?"
- **Doing:** Mentally scoring against the incumbent's actual output rather than
  against the other pitches; looking for the capability gap.
- **Pains:** Knowledge loss, transition cost, marginal-gain switching, being read
  as sentimental.
- **Gains:** A named capability the incumbent lacks, a real transition plan, an
  honest acknowledgment of what will be lost.

## Channels and content habits

He reads the capabilities and process pages and compares them line by line
against what he already gets. He looks specifically for the thing the incumbent
cannot do - a discipline, a speed, a market, a technical capability - and if he
does not find it he stops evaluating and starts defending. He notices whether the
studio acknowledges an incumbent exists; most pitches pretend otherwise, and he
reads that as either ignorance or bad manners.

## Decision-making process

No vote, meaningful drag exercised through the debrief and through the scope
conversation afterward. His threshold is a specific, named capability gap plus a
credible transition plan. Risk tolerance is low on continuity and moderate
elsewhere. He decides during the pitch and is persuadable by exactly one thing: a
capability the incumbent structurally cannot supply.

## Buying or action history

He defended the incumbent through a previous review and was largely vindicated
when the relationship improved. He also supported bringing in a specialist studio
for one discipline the incumbent genuinely could not do, and that arrangement
worked, which is the shape he is most likely to advocate for again: a carve-out,
not a replacement. Studios should note that this is his real influence - he
converts an award into a smaller award.

## Voice notes

Even, reasonable, hard to argue with because he is not being unreasonable. He
concedes points readily and returns to his one question. He does not disparage
the pitching studio and often compliments them sincerely on the way to declining.

Actual phrases: "That's good work. Our current team could also do that." "What's
the thing you can do that they can't?" "Nobody costs the six months." "I'm not
against this. I want to know what we're buying that we don't have."

## Boundaries and likely no

He will not support a switch for a marginal improvement in a capability the
incumbent already possesses. He will not accept a fast-ramp claim without a
transition plan. He is genuinely convertible on a named structural gap, and he is
also convertible to a partial award, which is the outcome he most often produces.
A pitch that never acknowledges the incumbent exists gets a firm, courteous no
from this seat.

## Modeled assumptions

- Assumption: An incumbent agency exists. For a greenfield pitch, deactivate this
  seat rather than reinterpreting it.
- Assumption: A long, generally positive incumbent relationship. If the incumbent
  is being fired for cause, this seat inverts and should be rewritten.
- Assumption: That his influence expresses as scope reduction rather than as
  award blocking.
- Calibration note: Record whether real awards came in smaller than pitched. That
  is the direct test of this seat's mechanism.
